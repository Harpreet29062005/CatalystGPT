Rethinking the Role of Homogeneous Surface Models in High-
Entropy Alloy Catalyst Screening
Jin-Soo Kim1‡, Taegyeong Kim1‡, Youngtak Kim1, Sathya Sheela Subramanian1, Geun Ho Gu1*
1Department of Energy Engineering, Korea Institute of Energy Technology, Naju 58330, Republic of
Korea
Abstract
High-entropy alloy (HEA) catalysts are commonly modeled as homogeneously mixed surfaces,
although thermodynamic driving forces can produce surface segregation and chemical ordering.
Here, we test the validity and practical limits of this approximation by comparing random
homogeneous slabs with surfaces annealed using cluster-expansion Monte Carlo (CEMC) against
three published high-throughput composition-activity datasets for the oxygen reduction and
hydrogen evolution reactions. Monte Carlo-based uncertainty quantification simulations
propagated uncertainties in catalyst composition and predicted binding energies through 10,000
trials. CEMC generally improved agreement with experiment, but the advantage is not robust, and
neither model reliably reproduces experimental composition-activity map. Structural analysis
revealed surface segregation, rather than short-range ordering, is the primary origin of the
difference between the models. Nevertheless, the element-conditioned binding-energy ranges of
the two models largely overlapped, supporting the use of homogeneous slabs for rapid screening
of elemental combinations.
Keywords: High-entropy alloys, Surface segregation, Thermodynamic surface reconstruction,
Catalysis, Monte Carlo annealing, Adsorption energy modeling, Combinatorial catalysis, Machine
learning in materials science

Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have emerged as an effective materials platform for catalyst
discovery owing to their compositional flexibility and the resulting diverse binding sites.1–4 HEAs
incorporate multiple elements within a single crystalline phase, providing access to a near-
continuous spectrum of adsorption energies, enabling systematic exploration of catalytic trends
across a broad compositional space.5–12 The energetic diversity is expected to increase the
likelihood of achieving adsorption strength near optimal values, consistent with the Sabatier
principle,13 and has motivated extensive theoretical6–9,14–17, and high-throughput experimental
efforts10,15,18–22 to accelerate catalyst design.
Despite these advances, predictive modeling of HEA catalysts has largely relied on the
assumption of homogeneous atomic mixing6–9,11,12,14,23–25, effectively treating the surface as a
direct extension of the bulk composition. While this approximation simplifies the representation
of multicomponent systems, it neglects the thermodynamic driving forces that promote surface
segregation and short-range ordering. Both computational studies26–30 and emerging atomic-scale
characterization31–34 have shown that multi-metallic systems frequently deviate from random
configurations, particularly at surfaces where differences in elemental surface energies reshape
local environments. Recent computational work35 has begun to incorporate segregation-driven
surface structures into catalytic modeling, but their predictive performance has not yet been
systematically evaluated through comparison with composition-dependent experimental activity
data. Consequently, the validity of the homogeneous surface approximation for catalyst screening
remains unresolved.
These observations raise two questions for computational catalyst discovery: First, are
catalytic predictions governed by the nominal bulk composition of a high-entropy alloy, or by the
thermodynamically selected surface state that emerges under realistic conditions? Second, if a
homogeneously mixed surface model fails to predict composition-dependent experimental
catalytic activity, does this mean that the homogeneous surface model is entirely useless for
computational catalyst screening? Resolving these questions is critical, as most computational
screening frameworks assume homogeneously mixed surfaces despite the expectation of surface
segregation and short-range ordering in multicomponent alloys. More broadly, this issue extends

beyond high-entropy alloys to multicomponent catalysts, where surface composition and local
atomic environments are set by thermodynamic equilibration rather than nominal composition.
Cluster-expansion Monte Carlo (CEMC) can provide thermodynamically realistic surface
configurations by accounting for surface segregation and chemical ordering.35 However,
experimentally synthesized HEA surfaces may retain kinetically trapped configurations rather than
reaching complete thermodynamic equilibrium, and the extent of atomic rearrangement during
synthesis and cooling remains uncertain.34,36 Moreover, activity predictions based on CEMC-
annealed structures still depend on approximate binding energy (BE) prediction models and
simplified Sabatier relationships for activity prediction. A direct comparison with experiment is
therefore required to establish both the capabilities and the limitations of these computational
approaches.
Here, we compare CEMC-annealed surfaces with homogeneous mixing slabs using
published high-throughput composition–activity datasets for the oxygen reduction reaction
(ORR)19,37 and hydrogen evolution reactions (HER)15. Because both the measured catalyst
compositions and predicted BE are subject to uncertainty relative to their true values, we evaluated
the models using MC-based uncertainty quantification (UQ), which is the systematic propagation
of uncertainties in model inputs into distributions of predicted outputs and model-performance
metrics.38 In the present study, UQ is used to determine whether the apparent difference between
the two surface models remains meaningful after uncertainties in catalyst composition and BE
prediction are considered.
We find that the homogeneously mixed slab model generally fails to reproduce the
experimentally observed variation in catalytic activity with composition. Incorporating
thermodynamically driven segregation through CEMC improves agreement with experiment,
indicating that surface redistribution is important for describing composition-dependent catalytic
behavior. Nevertheless, CEMC-annealed structures combined with BE prediction models and
Sabatier-based activity prediction models do not fully reproduce the experimental activity
landscapes. At present, neither approach provides a sufficiently reliable substitute for experiments
when predicting activity changes caused by variations in atomic ratio within a fixed multielement
system. Despite this limitation, homogeneous slabs may retain practical value at an earlier stage

of catalyst discovery: they can be used to screen whether a given combination of elements can
provide binding environments near the catalytic optimum. This distinction defines a useful
boundary for homogeneous models—they may assist in selecting constituent elements, but they
should not be expected to reliably optimize their atomic ratios.
Results
Assessment of Annealed and Homogeneous Surface Models with Uncertainty Quantification
The atomic-scale structure of the HEA surfaces investigated here—whether random or chemically
ordered—remains uncertain. Among the three experimental studies considered, studies 119 and 237
synthesized the HEA catalysts by magnetron sputtering without intentional heating or cooling,
suggesting relatively slow cooling that may allow partial annealing and structural equilibration,
whereas study 315 employed rapid cooling from 2023 K to room temperature within 5 s. Previous
experimental and kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) studies provide contrasting indications regarding
the structures retained after cooling. Kim et al.34 used scanning tunneling microscopy to observe
local chemical order at the surface of a CoCrFeMnNi HEA that was water-cooled from 3500 K to
room temperature. In contrast, the KMC simulations of Du et al.36 indicated that bulk crystal of
CrCoNi medium-entropy alloys can retain their high-temperature structures when cooled rapidly
from 1500 to 600 K over 10 s. Whether atomic rearrangement occurs on comparable timescales
remains unclear especially for surface; the open surface may facilitate faster atomic diffusion and
thereby accelerate ordering.
Accordingly, we evaluated whether CEMC-annealed slabs or randomly mixed
homogeneous slabs more accurately reproduce the experimentally measured catalytic activities,
while leaving the determination of the exact kinetically trapped structures outside the scope of this
study. For the CEMC model, the annealing temperature was selected based on the best agreement
according to Kendall’s 𝜏 describing the rank order, mean squared error (MSE) assessing the

From a catalyst screening perspective, the ability to identify promising compositions is
important. Thus, we present the continuous recall analysis for three studies, which were obtained
using this most probable trial in each model, as shown in Figure S11-S12. For example, when the
top 20% of samples are selected from the entire compositions based on experimental activity, recall
is defined as the fraction of those samples whose predicted activities also fall within the top 20%.
CEMC achieved slightly higher recall than the homogeneous model in Study 1; ML1, ML3, ML4,
and ML6 of Study 2; and the FCC(100) hollow site of Study 3. In study 1 (Figure S11f), recall of
homogeneous model approaches to the random baseline. However, in study 2 (Figure S10), the
homogeneous model often achieves recall values above the baseline, and the difference between

CEMC and the homogeneous model is small. In study 3 (Figure S11a-e), except for the FCC (100)
hollow site, both CEMC and the homogeneous model perform similarly to the random baseline.
This may suggest that the FCC (100) hollow site is the active site of the FeCoNiPdPt HEA catalyst,
and the homogeneous model may fail to screen promising composition ratios at this site. The
activity maps reveal persistent systematic errors in both models, particularly the overestimation of
experimentally low-activity compositions. CEMC more closely reproduces some experimental
trends than the homogeneous model, but substantial discrepancies remain for both approaches. We
therefore next examine the structural origin of CEMC’s relative advantage and the potential role
of homogeneous slabs in catalyst screening.
Surface segregation rather than local ordering reshapes the active-site landscape Performing
CEMC simulations results in higher surface fractions of Pd and Pt than those observed in
homogeneous slabs in PtPdIrRhRu system, and higher Pd fraction in FeCoNiPdPt system, as
shown in Figure 3a and 3b. The temperature-dependent compositional evolution (Figure 3a and
3b) shows that the outermost layer is dominated by Pd and Pt, indicating strong surface enrichment
of elements associated with favorable segregation energetics. At elevated temperature, the surface
retains partial mixing, reflecting the stabilizing contribution of configurational entropy. Upon
cooling to 298 K, however, the surface undergoes pronounced chemical stratification, becoming
strongly enriched in Pd and/or Pt, while the rest are largely excluded from the surface layer.
Figure S13 shows the layer-resolved composition of the CEMC-annealed slab at 298 K
for the equi-atomic PtPdIrRhRu fcc(111), FeCoNiPdPt fcc(100), FeCoNiPdPt fcc(110), and
FeCoNiPdPt fcc(111) surfaces. In PtPdIrRhRu system, the second layer becomes Ru- and Rh-rich
(36.5, 30.7%, respectively) with minor contribution of Pd (12.5%), Ir (10.6%), and Pt (9.7%).
Third and fourth layers display Ir-, Ru-, and Rh-rich compositions, while Pd and Pt contribution
is small. In the 5th layers, the Pd, Pt, and Rh fractions are close to their bulk values, whereas Ru
and Ir are slightly depleted relative to the bulk composition. These trends indicate that surface
segregation of Pd and Pt, and subsurface segregation of Ru and Rh after CEMC. In the FeCoNiPdPt
system, the second layer of all three facets displays Fe-rich composition, followed by Pt
contribution on fcc(100) and fcc(110), and Co contribution on fcc(111). In the third and fourth

layers of all three facets, elements that were minor constituents of the surface and subsurface layers
become dominant. Because Pd dominates the surface layer, it becomes a minor constituent in the
second through fifth layers. Across all three facets, Co and Ni show a tendency to occupy third
layer and deeper layers.

This redistribution reshapes the adsorption-energy landscape (Figure 4). As shown in
Figure 4a, whereas homogeneous surfaces exhibit a broad multimodal distribution, CEMC
annealing produces dominant peak near 1.0 and 1.2 eV, close to the Sabatier optimum for OH
adsorption and primarily associated with Pt- and Pd-containing ensembles. Consequently, the
predicted activity increases, from 0.047 to 0.14. This contraction indicates that thermodynamic
reconstruction selectively amplifies catalytically favorable motifs while suppressing unfavorable
configurations. Randomly shuffling atoms within each CEMC layer, which removes local ordering
while preserving surface segregation, produces a similar BE distribution. Thus, the differences
between the CEMC and homogeneous models arise primarily from surface segregation rather than
surface ordering.
As shown in Figure 4b, the H-BE distributions associated with the different elements have
similar peak heights and positions, on homogeneous FeCoNiPdPt FCC(100) hollow site. CEMC
annealing amplifies distribution associated with Pd-containing ensembles, thereby the predicted
activity increases, from 0.088 to 0.19. Layer shuffled CEMC slab displays slightly different
distribution with slightly increased activity of 0.038. The H-BE distributions for the remaining
facet/site combinations of the FeCoNiPdPt system are presented in Figure S14 and show trends
similar to those observed for the FCC(100) hollow site. Thus, surface segregation alters the BE
distributions, whereas surface ordering appears to have only a minor effect.

Is homogeneous slab model useful for screening? Although the homogeneous slab model is less
accurate than the CEMC-annealed slab model in predicting changes in catalytic activity with
elemental fractions within a given compositional system, it may still be useful for selecting, for
example, five elements for an HEA catalyst from a broader candidate pool, such as 20 transition
metals. Selecting five elements from 20 yields 15,504 possible combinations, and screening these
combinations using homogeneous slabs is substantially faster than performing MC annealing for
every candidate system. The number of active sites with BEs close to the optimum does not change
substantially relative to that in the homogeneous slab, although CEMC alters the elemental
composition of the surface. If an active site is defined as a site with a BE within ±0.1 eV of the
optimal value, the activity of a slab increases with the number of such sites. For example, for the
equi-atomic PtPdIrRhRu composition, Figure 5a shows the element-resolved OH BE distributions
for the homogeneous slab, and the corresponding activity volcano. Figure 5b exhibits the H-BE
distributions on the equi-atomic FeCoNiPdPt fcc(100) hollow site with activity volcano. Figure 5

indicates that only sites with BEs within 0.1 eV close to the optimal value contribute meaningfully
to the activity. Thus, the activity is primarily determined by whether sites with BEs close to the
optimal value are present.

Figure 7 illustrates the workflow of utilizing homogeneous slab for computational catalyst
screening. For example, to screen five-element combinations selected from 20 transition metals,
homogeneous slabs could be constructed for each combination at the equi-atomic composition.
After calculating the surface BE distributions, the number of active sites with BEs within ±0.1 eV
of the optimal value could be compared across the combinations. The combinations could then be
ranked by the number of active sites, providing guidance on which candidates should be prioritized
for further analysis to determine whether the elements associated with these active sites will
segregate to the actual surface. If these elements are confirmed to segregate to the surface, their
experimental synthesis could be considered. However, within a fixed five-element system, changes
in catalytic activity resulting from variations in the atomic fractions cannot currently be reliably
screened using either the homogeneous or CEMC model and must therefore be evaluated
experimentally. The remaining discrepancies for both models suggest that experimental activity
landscapes are influenced by effects not captured in the present framework.

Limitations and scope of the model Several approximations were adopted in the quantitative
description of the experimental system. Catalytic activity was estimated from thermodynamic
overpotentials derived from scaling relations and therefore neglects kinetic barriers and
uncertainties associated with the scaling relationships. Although recent study39 have shown that
coverage-dependent lateral interactions between adsorbates can affect composition–activity trends,
we assumed isolated adsorption sites to simplify the problem. According to Du et al.36, the degree
of ordering in bulk depends on the annealing duration requiring kinetics; however, the present
work only accounts for the thermodynamics. In PtPdIrRhRu system, calculations were restricted
to the (111) surface, whereas multiple facets may be present experimentally. The omission of

kinetic barriers, coverage-dependent adsorbate interactions, annealing kinetics, and
experimentally relevant facet distributions may therefore account for part of the remaining
discrepancy between the predicted and experimental activity landscapes.
Conclusions
We systematically evaluated whether thermodynamically annealed HEA surfaces reproduce
composition-dependent catalytic activity more accurately than conventionally assumed
homogeneous surfaces. Across the three experimental datasets,15,19,37 CEMC was generally more
likely to achieve higher τ and lower MSE and CRPS than the homogeneous model, with mean
probabilities of 0.60, 0.57, and 0.51, respectively. However, residual errors in the activity maps
and recall gains show that the improvement is limited; neither model yet reliably predicts
composition-activity relationship. CEMC revealed pronounced Pd and Pt surface enrichment and
selective short-range ordering. Layer-wise shuffling largely preserved the BE distributions,
demonstrating that surface segregation, rather than short-range ordering, is the primary origin of
the differences between the two models for describing activity. Nevertheless, homogeneous slabs
captured most element-conditioned BE ranges and may therefore remain useful for rapid, early-
stage screening of elemental combinations. While the considered experiments exposed the active
sites, the screened candidate may not segregate the elemental dependent active sites, thus surface
thermodynamics is a critical final step in HEA screening. Our results point that the experiments
remain necessary for quantitative composition optimization. The limited absolute agreement of
both models indicates that additional physical effects beyond thermodynamic surface
reconstruction must be incorporated to achieve quantitatively reliable activity predictions.
